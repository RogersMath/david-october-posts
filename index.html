Mon Oct 5

How many people have a wound at any one time?

A study of Medicare claims from 2014 looked at a 5% sample of beneficiaries.

Nearly 15% of them, about 8.2 million people when scaled up, had at least one wound or wound infection. The list covered pressure injuries, diabetic foot ulcers, venous ulcers, surgical wounds and more.

This is a retrospective claims analysis, so it counts billed episodes. It does not count wounds that never reached a claim.

That is a lot of dressings, and a lot of decisions about which ones to use.

Source: Nussbaum et al., Value in Health, 2018.

Wed Oct 7

Where Medicare wound dollars go

Monday's post covered how many Medicare beneficiaries have a wound. The same analysis also estimated what that costs.

Total Medicare spending on wounds was put between $28.1 and $96.8 billion, a wide range because of how episodes were counted. Surgical wounds were the most expensive type, followed by diabetic foot ulcers.

By setting, hospital outpatient care came first ($9.9 to $35.8 billion), then hospital inpatient care ($5.0 to $24.3 billion).

These are Medicare-only figures built from 2014 claims data.

Source: Nussbaum et al., Value in Health, 2018;21(1):27-32.

Fri Oct 9

The pig study behind moist wound healing

In 1962, George Winter published a short paper in Nature.

He made shallow wounds on the backs of young pigs. Some were left open to the air. Others were covered with a plastic film that kept them moist.

The covered wounds grew new skin faster. The paper is widely cited as the start of modern moist wound care, and the idea that a moist environment helps skin regrow now sits behind most modern dressings.

Source: Winter, Nature, 1962;193:293-294.

Mon Oct 12

Heel pressure injuries in intensive care: a dressing trial

Heel pressure injuries are a known risk for patients in intensive care.

A prospective study compared 150 ICU patients who got a multilayer silicone foam dressing on the heels with 152 who got standard care. No heel pressure injuries developed in the dressing group. In the standard care group, 14 patients developed a total of 19.

The authors concluded the dressing was clinically effective in reducing ICU-acquired heel pressure injuries.

Source: Santamaria et al., Journal of Wound Care, 2015;24(8):340-345.

#PressureInjury #WoundCare #CriticalCare

Wed Oct 14

A number that predicts diabetic foot ulcer healing

A study followed 203 people with diabetic foot ulcers in a randomized trial. Wounds were measured at the start and again at four weeks.

Among ulcers that healed by week 12, the area had shrunk by 82% at week four. Among ulcers that did not heal, it had shrunk by 25%.

The authors called percent change in area at four weeks a robust predictor of healing at twelve weeks.

A simple measurement, taken on a schedule, flagged which wounds were headed for trouble.

Source: Sheehan et al., Diabetes Care, 2003.

Receipts

Fri Oct 16

Silver dressings and venous leg ulcers

A 2018 Cochrane network meta-analysis compared dressings and topical agents for venous leg ulcers.

It found evidence that silver dressings may raise the chance of healing compared with nonadherent dressings (risk ratio 2.43, 95% CI 1.58 to 3.74). The authors rated that finding moderate-certainty.

The same review names compression as first-line treatment, with the dressing doing its work on top of it.

Source: Norman et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2018.

#WoundCare #VenousUlcer #Evidence

Mon Oct 19

Dressing changes hurt more than many people expect

An international survey asked 2,018 people with chronic wounds, across 15 countries, about pain.

At dressing changes, 31.9% had pain most or all of the time. Average pain intensity was 44.5 on a 100-point scale. And 40.3% named dressing-change pain as the worst part of living with a chronic wound. Touching or handling the wound hurt the most.

This was a one-time survey of self-reported pain. It did not compare dressing types.

Pain is something patients can report, and it is worth asking about at every visit.

Source: Price et al., International Wound Journal, 2008;5(2):159-171.

Sacral dressings and pressure injuries: what the pooled trials show

A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled six randomized trials with 1,872 participants.

Protective sacral dressings reduced pressure injury risk by about 70% overall, and by 83% in intensive care settings. The authors rated this as moderate evidence.

Pressure injuries on the sacrum are common and costly, so a prevention step with pooled trial support is worth knowing about.

Source: Fulbrook, Mbuzi and Miles, Int J Nurs Stud, 2019;100:103400.

#PressureInjury #WoundCare #Prevention
Receipts

Fri Oct 23

Biofilm in chronic wounds: how common is it?

A 2017 meta-analysis pulled together published studies that tested human chronic wounds for biofilm.

Nine studies with 185 wounds met the criteria. Biofilm was found in 78.2% of them (confidence interval 61.6 to 89).

The sample is small, and the studies used different detection methods. The authors still concluded that biofilm is very common in chronic non-healing wounds.

That is the reason cleansing and debridement get so much attention in wound care guidance.

Source: Malone et al., J Wound Care, 2017;26(1):20-25.

#WoundCare #Biofilm #ChronicWounds
Receipts

Adhesive skin injury is common and often missed

A panel of 23 experts met to write consensus statements on medical adhesive-related skin injury, often shortened to MARSI.

They described it as prevalent but underrecognized, and said it occurs across all age groups and care settings. Their statements cover assessment, prevention and treatment.

Consensus statements are a lower tier of evidence than trials. They are a record of expert agreement.

Source: McNichol et al., J Wound Ostomy Continence Nurs, 2013;40(4):365-380.

Wed Oct 28

Compression is the foundation of venous ulcer care

A Cochrane review looked at compression for venous leg ulcers. Evidence from eight randomized trials showed better healing outcomes, including time to healing, when patients received compression compared with none.

The authors concluded that compression increases ulcer healing rates.

The dressing sits on top of a plan that starts there.

Source: O'Meara et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2012.

#WoundCare #VenousUlcer #Compression
Receipts
  1. O'Meara S, Cullum N, Nelson EA, Dumville JC. Compression for venous leg ulcers. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2012;(11):CD000265.
Fri Oct 30

After surgery, scar prevention is part of wound care

Updated practical guidelines on scar management were published in 2014 in the Journal of Plastic, Reconstructive and Aesthetic Surgery.

The authors describe silicone sheets and gels as universally considered the first-line option to prevent and treat hypertrophic scars, and call it a gold-standard, non-invasive therapy.

For wound care teams, that puts silicone at the front of the line once a surgical wound has closed.

Source: Monstrey et al., J Plast Reconstr Aesthet Surg, 2014;67(8):1017-1025.

Vet 1

Horse wounds heal more slowly on the lower leg

A review of second-intention healing in horses and ponies reports a pattern: repair is faster in ponies than in horses, and faster in body wounds than in limb wounds.

An earlier histology study compared horse and pony wounds directly. Limb wounds in horses are also prone to exuberant granulation tissue, often called proud flesh.

So location and species both change how a wound behaves. A plan that works on a pony's body may not match a horse's lower leg.

Sources: Wilmink and van Weeren, 2005. Wilmink et al., Equine Vet J, 1999.

Vet 2

A horse study on silicone dressings

Limb wounds are among the hardest to manage in horses, partly because of exuberant granulation tissue, often called proud flesh.

A 2005 study put a silicone-containing dressing on experimentally made limb wounds in five horses. Ten wounds got the silicone dressing and ten got a conventional dressing.

The silicone dressing inhibited the formation of exuberant granulation tissue. The authors suggested it could be part of a plan to improve the repair of limb wounds in horses.

Source: Ducharme-Desjarlais et al., Am J Vet Res, 2005;66(7):1133-1139.

Vet 3

What horse wound research focuses on

A 2024 scoping review pulled together 81 publications on treating skin wounds in horses.

The distal limbs were the most common injury sites, and visual inspection was the most common way to judge healing. The authors called for more work toward standardized, evidence-based protocols.

That gives veterinary teams a clear picture of where the research is concentrated: lower-leg wounds, judged by eye.

Source: Ribeiro et al., Animals, 2024;14(10):1500.

Vet 4

How often bandages cause problems in cats

A multi-institution study reviewed records of 152 cats that had a bandage after a clean orthopaedic injury.

Complications were reported in 104 cats (68.4%). Most were mild (64.4%), 32.7% were moderate and 2.9% were severe. Splints predicted more severe complications.

The authors urged careful case selection and monitoring. It is a retrospective record review, so it shows how often problems were recorded, not why.

Source: Clayton et al., Vet Comp Orthop Traumatol, 2026;39(2):126-132.

Vet 5

Infection after eye removal in dogs

A retrospective study at one veterinary teaching hospital looked at surgical site infection after enucleation in 280 dogs.

The infection rate was 5%. No risk factors were identified that could guide prevention.

That last line matters. A rare complication can still lack a clear fix, and the authors said so.

It is one study of one procedure at one hospital, and it does not speak to other surgeries.

Source: Dacanay et al., Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2023.

Vet 6

A moist dressing and surgical wounds in dogs

A study in the Australian Veterinary Journal tested a hydrocolloid dressing on surgical wounds in six female dogs after ovariohysterectomy.

The dressing stayed on well, stayed flexible, and kept the wound from becoming contaminated. It improved the granulation rate.

The authors described it as an active wound management tool that uses moist wound conditions, where conventional bandages and gauze mainly protect.

Source: Abramo et al., Aust Vet J, 2008;86(3):95-99.

Which scar treatment do the guidelines list first?

Updated practical guidelines for scar management came out in 2014 from a group of plastic surgery researchers.

Silicone sheets and gels are listed first among the non-invasive options. The authors describe silicone as universally considered the first-line prophylactic and treatment option for hypertrophic scars, and call it a gold-standard, non-invasive therapy.

For surgeons planning scar care, the guideline starting point is clear.

What an international scar panel based its advice on

A 2014 international panel updated its scar management recommendations in Dermatologic Surgery.

The recommendations came from a comprehensive literature search plus the clinical experience of the panel. The authors point to a growing body of research behind newer options such as laser therapy, and conclude that scar care works best when it is individualized.

Source: Gold et al., Dermatol Surg, 2014;40(8):825-831.

Plastics 3

Tension and scars: a within-patient trial

A randomized trial in 65 healthy adults compared scars after abdominoplasty. One half of each incision got a device that reduces tension on the wound. The other half got the surgeon's usual care.

At 12 months, the treated side scored better on scar appearance (2.90 vs 3.29 on a visual scale, where lower is better). No serious adverse events were reported.

The design is strong because each patient served as their own control.

Source: Longaker et al., Plast Reconstr Surg, 2014;134(3):536-546.

#PlasticSurgery #ScarManagement #Abdominoplasty
Receipts
  1. Longaker MT, Rohrich RJ, Greenberg L, et al. A randomized controlled trial of the embrace Advanced Scar Therapy device to reduce incisional scar formation. Plast Reconstr Surg. 2014;134(3):536-546.
Plastics 4

How scars get measured in studies

Scar studies need a way to score a scar. One widely used tool is the Patient and Observer Scar Assessment Scale, or POSAS.

It has two parts: one filled in by the patient and one by a clinician. A 2004 study in Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery described it as a reliable and feasible tool for scar evaluation.

When a trial reports scar results, the scale used and who did the scoring are worth checking.

Source: Draaijers et al., Plast Reconstr Surg, 2004;113(7):1960-1965.

Plastics 5

Moist dressings for skin graft donor sites

A 2018 systematic review looked at dressings for split-thickness skin graft donor sites. It included 41 articles comparing 44 dressings and measured pain, healing rate, infection, appearance and cost.

Moist dressings resulted in lower pain, and 73% of articles reported better re-epithelialization with moist dressings.

The authors call for standardized research to pin down the best option, and the moist-dressing results are the clearest signal so far.

Source: Serebrakian et al., Plast Reconstr Surg Glob Open, 2018;6(9):e1928.

Plastics 6

Silicone gel and sternotomy scars: a double-blind trial

A randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind trial tested topical silicone gel on median sternotomy scars, the incision down the chest for heart surgery.

It included 50 patients and 100 wounds. At three months, treated wounds scored better on pigmentation (p = 0.02), vascularity (p = 0.001) and pliability (p = 0.001). No adverse effects were reported, and 98% of patients stuck with the treatment.

The authors called the effect promising for preventing hypertrophic scars after surgery.

Source: Chan et al., Plast Reconstr Surg, 2005;116(4):1013-1020.

#PlasticSurgery #ScarManagement #SiliconeGel
Receipts
  1. Chan KY, Lau CL, Adeeb SM, Somasundaram S, Nasir-Zahari M. A randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, prospective clinical trial of silicone gel in prevention of hypertrophic scar development in median sternotomy wound. Plast Reconstr Surg. 2005;116(4):1013-1020.
