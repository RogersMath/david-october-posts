Mon Oct 5

How many people have a wound at any one time?

A study of Medicare claims from 2014 looked at a 5% sample of beneficiaries.

Nearly 15% of them, about 8.2 million people when scaled up, had at least one wound or wound infection. The list covered pressure injuries, diabetic foot ulcers, venous ulcers, surgical wounds and more.

This is a retrospective claims analysis, so it counts billed episodes. It does not count wounds that never reached a claim.

That is a lot of dressings, and a lot of decisions about which ones to use.

Source: Nussbaum et al., Value in Health, 2018.

Wed Oct 7

Where the money in wound care actually goes

A UK study costed wound care for one health district of 488,000 people in 2006-2007.

The total was £9.89 million, or 1.44% of the local health budget. The authors named the biggest pieces as hospital stays and nurse time.

One more number: 30.7% of hospital inpatients in that survey had a wound.

One limit to keep in mind. It is a single district in one year, and one author works for a dressing maker.

Source: Vowden, Vowden and Posnett, J Wound Care, 2009.

Fri Oct 9

The pig study behind moist wound healing

In 1962, George Winter published a short paper in Nature.

He made shallow wounds on the backs of young pigs. Some were left open to the air. Others were covered with a plastic film that kept them moist.

The covered wounds grew new skin faster. This work is widely cited as the start of modern moist wound care.

It was done in pigs, on shallow wounds. Later human trials had to test whether the idea held up in people.

Source: Winter, Nature, 1962;193:293-294.

Mon Oct 12

A number that predicts diabetic foot ulcer healing

A study followed 203 people with diabetic foot ulcers in a randomized trial. Wounds were measured at the start and again at four weeks.

Among ulcers that healed by week 12, the area had shrunk by 82% at week four. Among ulcers that did not heal, it had shrunk by 25%.

The authors called percent change in area at four weeks a robust predictor of healing at twelve weeks.

A simple measurement, taken on a schedule, flagged which wounds were headed for trouble.

Source: Sheehan et al., Diabetes Care, 2003.

Wed Oct 14

What the Cochrane review says about dressings and surgical site infection

Cochrane reviewers pooled randomized trials of dressings on surgical wounds that were closed at the time of surgery.

They found no clear evidence that one dressing type beat another at preventing surgical site infection. They also found no clear evidence that covering the wound at all lowered the risk. Most trials were small, and the certainty of the evidence was rated low or very low.

The reviewers suggested basing the choice on dressing cost and patient preference.

So the evidence supports picking a dressing by how it performs for the patient, not by a promise about infection.

Source: Dumville et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2016, CD003091.

Fri Oct 16

Foam dressings and diabetic foot ulcers: what the trials show

A Cochrane review looked at foam dressings for diabetic foot ulcers.

In a meta-analysis of two studies, foam did not show a clear healing advantage over basic wound contact dressings (risk ratio 2.03, 95% CI 0.91 to 4.55). The interval crosses 1, so the result is inconclusive.

The authors found no research evidence that foam heals these ulcers better than other types, and noted that all trials were very small.

Absence of proof is not proof of absence. It does mean larger trials are needed.

Source: Dumville et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2013, CD009111.

Mon Oct 19

For venous leg ulcers, compression comes first

A Cochrane review of venous leg ulcer trials reached a plain conclusion: compression increases ulcer healing rates compared with no compression.

A separate 2018 Cochrane network meta-analysis looked at dressings and topical agents. It called compression the first-line treatment and concluded that more research is needed to learn whether particular dressings improve healing.

In short, the dressing sits under a treatment plan. It does not replace one.

Sources: O'Meara et al., Cochrane, 2012. Norman et al., Cochrane, 2018.

Wed Oct 21

Biofilm in chronic wounds: how common is it?

A 2017 meta-analysis pulled together published studies that tested human chronic wounds for biofilm.

Nine studies with 185 wounds met the criteria. Biofilm was found in 78.2% of them (confidence interval 61.6 to 89).

The sample is small, and the studies used different detection methods. The authors still concluded that biofilm is very common in chronic non-healing wounds.

That is the reason cleansing and debridement get so much attention in wound care guidance.

Source: Malone et al., J Wound Care, 2017;26(1):20-25.

Adhesive skin injury is common and often missed

A panel of 23 experts met to write consensus statements on medical adhesive-related skin injury, often shortened to MARSI.

They described it as prevalent but underrecognized, and said it occurs across all age groups and care settings. Their statements cover assessment, prevention and treatment.

Consensus statements are a lower tier of evidence than trials. They are a record of expert agreement.

Source: McNichol et al., J Wound Ostomy Continence Nurs, 2013;40(4):365-380.

Mon Oct 26

Do dressings on the sacrum prevent pressure injuries?

A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled six randomized trials with 1,872 participants.

It found moderate evidence that a protective sacral dressing can reduce pressure injury risk. The pooled estimate suggested about a 70% lower risk overall, and a larger drop in intensive care.

Some trials in this area were funded by dressing makers, so the estimate may be on the hopeful side. Wednesday's post covers a newer trial.

Source: Fulbrook, Mbuzi and Miles, Int J Nurs Stud, 2019;100:103400.

Wed Oct 28

A newer trial on sacral dressings found no benefit

The EEPOC trial randomized 958 at-risk medical-surgical adults in hospital. Half got a silicone foam border dressing on the sacrum plus routine care. Half got routine care alone. It was published in 2026.

The authors reported no evidence that the dressing reduced hospital-acquired sacral pressure injuries. The dressings also cost more than routine care alone.

This result differs from the 2019 meta-analysis. Different patients, settings and routine care can all change a result.

Both findings belong in the picture.

Source: Walker et al. (EEPOC), Int J Nurs Stud, 2026;173:105258.

Fri Oct 30

How to read a dressing network meta-analysis

The 2018 Cochrane review on dressings for venous leg ulcers used a network meta-analysis, which compares many treatments at once, even if they were never tested head to head.

One result: silver dressings may raise the chance of healing compared with nonadherent dressings (risk ratio 2.43, 95% CI 1.58 to 3.74). That finding was rated moderate-certainty, but inside a network the reviewers rated low-certainty overall.

The lesson is to read the certainty rating beside the number. A strong-looking ratio can sit inside weak evidence.

Source: Norman et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2018.

#WoundCare #EvidenceBasedMedicine #Research
Receipts
  1. Norman G, Westby MJ, Rithalia AD, et al. Dressings and topical agents for treating venous leg ulcers. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2018;(6):CD012583.

Vet 1

Horse wounds heal more slowly on the lower leg

A review of second-intention healing in horses and ponies reports a pattern: repair is faster in ponies than in horses, and faster in body wounds than in limb wounds.

An earlier histology study compared horse and pony wounds directly. Limb wounds in horses are also prone to exuberant granulation tissue, often called proud flesh.

So location and species both change how a wound behaves. A plan that works on a pony's body may not match a horse's lower leg.

Sources: Wilmink and van Weeren, 2005. Wilmink et al., Equine Vet J, 1999.

Vet 2

A small horse study on silicone dressings

A 2005 study tested a silicone-containing dressing on limb wounds in horses. Five horses received wounds on the metacarpus; ten wounds got the silicone dressing and ten got a conventional one.

The silicone dressing inhibited exuberant granulation tissue. The authors suggested it could be part of a plan for limb wounds.

The sample is five horses with experimentally made wounds. Real injuries vary more. It is a promising early signal, not a final answer.

Source: Ducharme-Desjarlais et al., Am J Vet Res, 2005;66(7):1133-1139.

Vet 3

Most horse wound treatments looked good in reports and flat in experiments

A 2024 scoping review collected 81 publications on treating skin wounds in horses.

All of the case reports, case series and clinical studies reported positive outcomes. Among the experimental studies, only 36% found a significant healing improvement over controls. The authors concluded there is a lack of evidence for adopting specific treatment protocols.

Case reports tend to describe what worked. Controlled studies show what happens against a comparison.

The gap is a reason to ask what kind of study sits behind a claim.

Source: Ribeiro et al., Animals, 2024;14(10):1500.

Vet 4

How often bandages cause problems in cats

A multi-institution study reviewed records of 152 cats that had a bandage after a clean orthopaedic injury.

Complications were reported in 104 cats (68.4%). Most were mild (64.4%), 32.7% were moderate and 2.9% were severe. Splints predicted more severe complications.

The authors urged careful case selection and monitoring. It is a retrospective record review, so it shows how often problems were recorded, not why.

Source: Clayton et al., Vet Comp Orthop Traumatol, 2025.

Vet 5

Infection after eye removal in dogs

A retrospective study at one veterinary teaching hospital looked at surgical site infection after enucleation in 280 dogs.

The infection rate was 5%. No risk factors were identified that could guide prevention.

That last line matters. A rare complication can still lack a clear fix, and the authors said so.

It is one study of one procedure at one hospital, and it does not speak to other surgeries.

Source: Dacanay et al., Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2023.

Vet 6

Human evidence and animal evidence do not swap

Take silicone dressings as an example. A Cochrane review of silicone gel sheeting in people rated the trials as poor quality and called the results unclear.

In horses, a five-horse study found a silicone dressing inhibited exuberant granulation tissue.

Different species, different wounds, different questions. Neither result stands in for the other.

For animal patients, the best evidence comes from studies in that species.

Sources: O'Brien and Jones, Cochrane. Ducharme-Desjarlais et al., 2005.

Plastics 1

Scar guidelines and Cochrane do not say the same thing

Updated scar management guidelines from 2014 describe silicone sheets and gels as the first-line option to prevent and treat hypertrophic scars.

A Cochrane review of silicone gel sheeting found the trials to be of poor quality and said it is unclear whether the sheets prevent abnormal scarring or treat it.

Both are true. Guidelines blend trials with clinical experience. Cochrane grades trial quality.

A useful reading is that silicone is a standard first step, and the trial evidence behind it is weaker than the standing it has.

Sources: Monstrey et al., 2014. O'Brien and Jones, Cochrane.

What an international scar panel based its advice on

A 2014 international panel updated its scar management recommendations in Dermatologic Surgery.

The authors say the recommendations came from a literature search plus the clinical experience and consensus opinion of panel members. They conclude that care should be individualized.

That tells a reader how to weigh it. It is expert consensus informed by evidence, not a set of trial results.

Source: Gold et al., Dermatol Surg, 2014;40:825.

Plastics 3

Tension and scars: a within-patient trial

A randomized trial in 65 healthy adults compared scars after abdominoplasty. One half of each incision got a device that reduces tension on the wound. The other half got the surgeon's usual care.

At 12 months, the treated side scored better on scar appearance (2.90 vs 3.29 on a visual scale, where lower is better). No serious adverse events were reported.

The design is strong because each patient served as their own control. The trial was open-label, so patients and doctors knew which side was treated.

Source: Longaker et al., Plast Reconstr Surg, 2014;134(3):536-546.

Plastics 4

How scars get measured in studies

Scar studies need a way to score a scar. One widely used tool is the Patient and Observer Scar Assessment Scale, or POSAS.

It has two parts: one filled in by the patient and one by a clinician. A 2004 study in Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery described it as a reliable and feasible tool for scar evaluation.

When a trial reports scar results, the scale used and who did the scoring are worth checking.

Source: Draaijers et al., Plast Reconstr Surg, 2004;113(7):1960-1965.

Plastics 5

No ideal skin graft donor-site dressing yet

A 2018 systematic review looked at dressings for split-thickness skin graft donor sites. It included 41 articles comparing 44 dressings and measured pain, healing rate, infection, appearance and cost.

Moist dressings came out ahead on pain, and most articles reported faster re-epithelialization with them. Even so, the authors concluded the literature has not yet identified an ideal dressing.

The choice still rests on the outcomes that matter most for the patient and the practice.

Source: Serebrakian et al., Plast Reconstr Surg Glob Open, 2018;6(9):e1928.

Plastics 6

Silicone sheeting in hot weather

A paper from Saudi Arabia described problems using silicone gel sheeting for hypertrophic scars in a hot climate.

That is useful context for patients in warm places. It is a small clinical report, not a controlled trial. Its patients and scar types may not match a general surgical population.

Heat, sweat and wear time can all change how well a product is tolerated, which is why tolerability data deserve a place beside efficacy data.

Source: Nikkonen, Pitkänen and Al-Qattan, Burns, 2001;27(5):498-501.
